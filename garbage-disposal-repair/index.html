Leaking under the sink: when to call right away.

A leak under the sink can come from three places: the sink flange at the top, the dishwasher hose connection on the side, or the unit body itself. A drip at the flange or a loose hose is usually fixable. Water seeping from the housing or the bottom seam means the unit has failed.

Run a dry paper towel around each joint after the sink has been used. If you cannot find the source, or the cabinet floor is soaking, call the 24/7 line. We offer same-day appointments when available, and after-hours help for active leaks, since water left under a cabinet can swell wood and damage flooring.

Humming, jammed or silent: reading the symptoms.

The sound your disposal makes tells us a lot before we open anything.

Humming but not spinning.

The motor has power but the flywheel is stuck, often from a bone, fruit pit or utensil. Many jams clear with the right tool, and no part needs to be replaced.

Completely silent.

Check the reset button on the bottom of the unit and the outlet or breaker. If it stays dead after a reset, the motor may be burned out.

Grinding or rattling.

Something hard is inside, or the grinding components are worn or loose.

Slow draining sink.

Waste may be packing the drain line past the disposal, not just the grinder.

Bad smell.

Food residue stuck on the chamber walls or in the baffle is the usual cause, and cleaning often solves it.

What to keep out of the disposal.

Grease and oils cool and harden in the drain line, where they catch other waste. Fibrous foods such as celery, corn husks and onion skins wrap around the shredder. Starchy items like rice, pasta and potato peels can swell into a paste.

Bones, fruit pits, coffee grounds in bulk and eggshells in quantity also cause trouble. Run cold water while the disposal works and for about 15 seconds after, so waste moves fully down the line. If the sink backs up despite that, our drain cleaning service can clear the pipe beyond the disposal.

How we find the problem.

We start with safety, then work from the simple causes toward the hard ones.

  1. 1

    Confirm power is off.

    We unplug the unit or shut off the breaker, never relying on a wall switch alone.

  2. 2

    Check for a jam.

    A hex socket in the bottom of many units lets us turn the flywheel by hand, and tongs can lift out foreign objects.

  3. 3

    Test the leak path.

    We dry every joint, run water and look at the sink flange, gasket, dishwasher inlet and drain connection.

  4. 4

    Test the motor.

    If the unit hums or stays silent after a reset, we check electrical supply and decide whether the motor is worth saving.

Dishwasher connection and a clean install.

If you have a dishwasher, its drain hose attaches to a port on the disposal. On a new unit, a plug inside that port must be knocked out, or the dishwasher will back up. It is a common installation miss, and one we check every time.

We also look at the sink drain trap and the line past it, since a repair is wasted if a partial clog is still sitting downstream.

Disposals and hard water in Palisades Park.

The Veolia Water New Jersey 2025 report lists hardness at 210 ppm as calcium carbonate, which it classes as very hard. That can leave mineral scale on faucets and in drain fittings. A disposal is less affected than a water heater, but chalky buildup around the flange and seals is common.

We do not diagnose health effects of water. Our concern is the plumbing, and in a borough with about 7,668 housing units, many with older kitchens, a quick check of the whole sink area often catches problems early. We also serve Fort Lee and nearby towns.
