Toilet overflowing: what to do first.

An overflow gets worse fast. Follow these steps until help arrives.

  1. 1

    Stop the refill.

    Reach behind the bowl and turn the supply valve clockwise. If it does not move, lift the flapper in the tank or prop up the float to stop the fill.

  2. 2

    Do not flush again.

    Another flush adds more water to a bowl that cannot drain.

  3. 3

    Contain the water.

    Use towels at the base and keep outlets, cords and power strips away from the wet floor.

  4. 4

    Check other drains.

    If sinks or tubs gurgle or back up as well, the blockage may be in the main line and not the toilet. Call the 24/7 line and tell us.

A clogged toilet or a bigger drain problem.

A single slow toilet is usually a local clog that a plunger or closet auger can reach. When the toilet clogs repeatedly, or other fixtures react when you flush, the blockage is further down the line.

We use a closet auger, which is shaped to protect the porcelain, before any drain machine. If the line itself is the issue, our drain cleaning team takes over with the right equipment. Wipes marketed as flushable, paper towels and feminine products are the usual culprits.

Running toilet and tank leaks.

A running toilet wastes water around the clock and often signals a worn part. These are the usual sources.

Worn flapper.

A warped or mineral-coated rubber flapper lets tank water slip into the bowl, so the fill valve keeps refilling.

Failing fill valve.

If water keeps running or the tank refills slowly or noisily, the fill valve is likely worn.

Float set too high.

Water spilling into the overflow tube means the level is above the right line.

Loose tank bolts or gaskets.

Drips at the tank-to-bowl joint come from worn rubber washers.

Cracked tank.

A hairline crack that weeps cannot be sealed for good. The tank or toilet must be replaced.

Rocking toilet, wax ring and flange.

A toilet seats on a wax ring that seals the bowl to the drain flange in the floor. If the toilet rocks or you see water at the base after flushing, the ring has failed or the flange is damaged. Never ignore it, since sewer gas and leaking water can reach the subfloor.

We lift the toilet, inspect the flange for cracks or a wrong height, replace the wax or gasket and reset the fixture. A flange that has rusted or broken needs a repair ring or replacement before the toilet goes back on. If the floor is soft, tell us.

Supply valves that will not shut off.

The small stop valve behind the toilet is what stops an emergency. If it is stuck, leaks around the stem or will not close fully, it should be replaced. Old multi-turn valves can seize because mineral scale builds up inside.

The Veolia Water New Jersey 2025 report lists hardness at 210 ppm as calcium carbonate, which it calls very hard. Scale can coat flappers, fill valves and stop valves, so these parts may wear and stick sooner than in softer water areas. We do not give lifespan numbers, but a worn stop valve is cheap to change before a leak forces the issue.

Toilet installation and replacement.

A planned swap is straightforward when the flange and floor are sound. We remove the old toilet, set a new wax ring or gasket, level the fixture, connect a new supply line and test the flush. Rough-in distance, bowl shape and seat height matter, so measure or let us check.

For larger bathroom work, see plumbing installation. In an older Palisades Park house, we often find a flange or supply that has aged beyond reuse, so we plan for a few extra parts.
