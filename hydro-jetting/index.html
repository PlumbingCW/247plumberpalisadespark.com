A drain backup that returns days after it was snaked is stressful, and a main line that is already overflowing cannot wait for morning. Our 24/7 emergency dispatch handles hydro jetting in Palisades Park, NJ. Until we arrive, stop using water, keep the area around the backed-up drain clear, and stay away from wet outlets or appliances.

Hydro jetting sends water at very high pressure through a hose with a nozzle that fires jets backward and sideways. The spray scrubs the inside of the pipe, cuts through roots and flushes grease and sludge toward the sewer, which a cable machine cannot do as completely.

Snaked it and it clogged again? That is a pipe wall problem.

A cable opens a hole through a clog, like drilling a straw through mud. The drain runs for a while, then the leftover buildup on the pipe wall catches new debris and the hole closes. That is why a line that clears and then backs up three or four weeks later is rarely fixed for good by another cable pass.

The technical fix is hydro jetting, which cleans the full circumference of the pipe. It does not repair a broken pipe, though, so we confirm the pipe is sound before we use it.

Sewer backup: what to do first.

If waste water is coming up in a tub, shower or floor drain, treat it as a sanitation problem as well as a plumbing one.

  1. 1

    Stop sending water down.

    No flushing, no laundry, no dishwasher. Everything you run adds to the backup.

  2. 2

    Keep people and pets out.

    Backed-up water can carry bacteria. Keep children out of the area and wash hands after any contact.

  3. 3

    Watch for electrical hazards.

    If water reaches outlets, a furnace, or a panel, do not step in it. Call the 24/7 line and, if you can do so safely from a dry spot, turn off power to that area.

Why we put a camera in before we jet.

High-pressure water in a cracked, collapsed or badly offset pipe can push soil into the line or widen a break. A camera on a push cable shows the pipe material, any sag, and whether roots have entered through a joint. That tells us if jetting is safe and where to point the nozzle.

If the picture shows a damaged line, we stop and talk through your options, including sewer line repair. You see the footage, so the decision is yours and not a sales pitch. Our sewer camera inspection page explains what the footage includes.

Roots, grease and scale: what jetting removes.

Different buildups call for different nozzle choices and pressure settings.

Tree roots.

Fine roots enter through joints in clay or old cast iron and grow into a net. A rotating nozzle with a cutting head clears them, though roots usually return if the joint stays open.

Kitchen grease.

Fat hardens on the pipe wall and narrows the bore. Hot, high-flow water softens it and carries it out.

Soap and mineral scale.

Tubs and laundry lines build a crust over time. Jetting scrapes it back to bare pipe.

Packed debris.

Wipes, paper and hair that bind into a mat respond to a forward-firing nozzle.

Sludge in low spots.

A sagging section holds a pool of sediment. Jetting pushes it out, but the sag remains.

What affects the price of hydro jetting.

Jetting uses specialized equipment and time, so cost depends on the line. You get a clear price before work starts.

Length and size of the line.

A short branch costs less labor than a long main with several bends.

Access point.

A cleanout makes setup quick. Working through a toilet or roof vent takes longer.

Amount and type of buildup.

Light scale is fast. Packed roots or heavy grease takes multiple passes.

Camera inspection.

Looking first adds time but protects the pipe and can prevent wasted work.

Timing.

After-hours and weekend jobs can cost more than a daytime appointment.
