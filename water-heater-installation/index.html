Water heater installation in Palisades Park, NJ means setting a new unit in place, connecting water and fuel or power, venting it correctly and testing it before you rely on it. Doing it on your schedule beats doing it with water on the floor. A good job starts with choosing the right unit for your home, then getting every connection right.

This page covers sizing, expansion tanks, venting, relocation, permits and moving from gas to a heat pump. Call the 24/7 line to book a visit and get a clear price before work starts.

Sizing and picking a unit.

The label on a tank shows its capacity in gallons, but what matters is how fast it recovers and how much hot water your household uses at the busiest hour. A couple in a condo and a family sharing two bathrooms need different units.

We ask about showers, laundry habits and how many fixtures run at once, then match a unit to those answers. Oversizing wastes energy and floor space, and undersizing means cold showers. If you are comparing a tank against a demand-style heater, see our tankless water heater page.

What goes into a proper installation.

The tank is only part of the job. These are the pieces that make it safe and reliable.

Shutoff valves.

A working shutoff on the cold supply lets anyone isolate the heater in seconds during a leak.

Temperature and pressure relief valve.

This safety valve opens if pressure or heat climb too high. Its discharge line must be piped to a safe location.

Flexible or rigid connectors.

We choose connectors that suit the water lines and unit, using dielectric fittings where dissimilar metals meet.

Drain pan or leak control.

In a finished area, a pan with a drain path limits the damage from a future leak.

Venting or electrical supply.

Gas units need correct flue sizing and clearance, and electric units need a circuit that matches the manufacturer's requirements.

Expansion tanks and closed systems.

When a house has a check valve or pressure-reducing valve on the incoming line, heated water has nowhere to expand. Pressure climbs, which strains the tank and fixtures. An expansion tank gives that extra volume somewhere to go.

Whether you need one depends on how your system is set up, and we check that on site. If an old heater ran without one for years, the new install is a good time to look at the supply side. Our technician will test static pressure and explain what we find.

Venting, clearance and where the heater lives.

Gas and some power-vent units need a path for exhaust gases. The right flue size, slope and clearance from combustibles come from the manufacturer's instructions and the local rules, so we follow those rather than guess.

Moving a heater, for example from a crowded closet to a basement wall, changes pipe runs and sometimes venting. It can be worth it for access and service, but it adds work. Tell us what you want and we will lay out what the change involves. For related work see plumbing installation.

Moving from gas to a heat pump unit.

A heat pump water heater runs on electricity and pulls heat from the air, so the old gas line and flue are no longer used. It needs room for airflow, a condensate drain and a suitable electrical circuit. A basement or large utility room often works better than a small closet.

If you go this way, the gas supply must be capped properly and the old vent dealt with. We review the space, circuit and drain before suggesting it, and we tell you if a gas tank still makes more sense for your home.

Hard water and your new heater.

The Veolia Water New Jersey 2025 water quality report lists hardness at 210 ppm as calcium carbonate, which it classes as very hard. That means scale and sediment form faster in heaters. A new install is the moment to add isolation valves and plan a yearly flush.
