Koreatown is the name used for the Broad Avenue business district in Palisades Park, NJ. It is a commercial strip, not a residential subdivision. The borough's housing-planning document describes Korean stores and restaurants concentrated there. It calls the avenue "Koreatown/Korea Way."

This page is for the people who run and work in those businesses: restaurant owners, cafe managers, market operators, salon and shop tenants, and the landlords behind them. Plumbing realities for a food-and-retail strip.

The next points are general plumbing knowledge, not claims about any particular building on Broad Avenue. Kitchens put fat, oil and grease into drains all day. Where several units share a building drain, a blockage that starts in one kitchen can back up into a neighbor's floor drain or restroom.

Cafes and dessert shops have a different pattern. Sugary and starchy waste, coffee grounds and ice machine discharge can slow a small drain line over time. Karaoke venues and event spaces have the opposite problem: long quiet stretches, then a rush of restroom use. Fixtures that sit idle can dry out a trap and let sewer odor into the room.

If a drain is slow, early drain cleaning is cheaper than a weekend backup. When a cable leaves residue on the pipe wall, hydro jetting can scour the line, and a camera pass through sewer line cleaning shows whether the problem is grease, roots or a damaged section.

Problems we are called for on a commercial strip.

These are common patterns in food service and retail buildings in general.

Kitchen drain backing up.

Water pooling at a floor drain or prep sink during service often points to grease downstream of your own line.

Hidden leak and a high water bill.

A meter that keeps moving after close can mean a hidden leak. Leak detection traces it without opening every wall.

No hot water in the kitchen.

Dishwashing and handwashing depend on hot water. A failing tank or a tripped burner needs water heater repair quickly.

Restroom overflow.

A repeated toilet clog in a shared restroom is worth a line check instead of another plunge.

Hard water in a busy kitchen.

Veolia, the water utility serving the borough, reported a highest detected hardness of 210 ppm (as calcium carbonate) in its 2025 water quality report, which it classes as very hard. For a restaurant or cafe, hard water means mineral scale builds up inside water heaters, dishwashers, coffee equipment and ice machines.

Scale does not show up on a menu, but it shows up as a heater that takes longer to recover. We do not know the condition of any specific unit on Broad Avenue, so ask for a flush or inspection when a heater or hot line seems to struggle.

Permits and who may do the work.

Plumbing work in New Jersey must be done by a licensed master plumber or an authorized plumbing contractor, and the borough's Building Department handles plumbing permits. Fees are based on the number of fixtures or appliances. Plumbing inspections are held Tuesday and Thursday mornings, and an emergency repair needs its permit within 72 hours of the work.

Before any plumbing contractor starts, ask for their New Jersey license number and check it with the Division of Consumer Affairs. The Building Department can be reached at (201) 585-4108, and as a tenant you should also ask your landlord who holds responsibility for the building's lines.

What to do while you wait.

  1. 1

    Stop the water.

    Close the valve at the affected fixture, or the main shutoff if water is spreading.

  2. 2

    Keep people and power clear.

    Move staff and customers away from wet floors, and stay away from outlets and equipment standing in water.

  3. 3

    Stop using the drains.

    Do not flush or run sinks, because water has nowhere to go and may rise.

  4. 4

    Call and give the address.

    Tell the dispatcher your cross street, the floor, and whether lines are shared with another unit.
